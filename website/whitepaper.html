1 · Executive Summary

Omniverse Compute (OMC) is a decentralized physical infrastructure network (DePIN) that connects owners of idle GPU capacity with people who need compute: AI developers, Web3 and decentralized-AI projects, and rendering studios. Providers earn OMC and fees by running verified jobs. Requesters get access to GPU capacity without cloud quotas, long-term contracts or vendor lock-in.

OMC is built on three ideas:

  1. Honest scope. OMC targets workloads that distribute well across independent machines: inference, fine-tuning on a single node, batch rendering and other embarrassingly parallel jobs. It does not claim to replace tightly coupled data-center clusters for frontier-scale model training.
  2. Verification you can price. Every job is covered by a verification policy chosen by job type, combining spot-check re-execution, optional full redundancy, hardware-based confidential computing where available, and economic penalties through staking and slashing.
  3. Token demand tied to usage. Fees are paid per job, a share of every fee is used to buy back and burn OMC, and reward emissions are paid only against verified work.

Settlement and governance run on BNB Chain. High-frequency scheduling, heartbeats and micropayments run on a dedicated Layer 2 rollup built on the OP Stack (opBNB). Jobs execute off-chain in sandboxed containers on provider hardware.

2 · Problem and Opportunity

Demand. Generative AI has made GPU compute a scarce, expensive input. Small teams and independent developers face high hourly prices, regional quota limits and dependence on a few cloud vendors. Rendering studios face sharp, short-lived peaks in demand.

Supply. Large amounts of capability sit underused: consumer and workstation GPUs, idle capacity in small data centers, and hardware formerly used for proof-of-work mining.

Gap. There is no widely trusted way for strangers to rent each other GPU time with guarantees about correctness, data protection and payment. OMC aims to provide that missing layer.

4 · Architecture

4.1 Layers

L1 settlement (BNB Chain). OMC token contract, staking contracts, DAO governance, and final dispute arbitration.

L2 rollup orchestration. Node heartbeats, job allocation records, state-channel settlement and micropayments run on an OP Stack rollup on BNB Chain (opBNB). At launch a single sequencer operated by the project submits batches to L1. The published path to decentralization covers rotating sequencer sets, and the rollup's data-availability layer, challenge window, withdrawal delay and user exit procedure are documented in the technical documentation released with mainnet.

Off-chain compute grid. Provider nodes run jobs in isolated containers with CUDA or ROCm acceleration. Inputs and outputs move over encrypted peer-to-peer or relayed channels.

4.2 Job Lifecycle

  1. The requester submits a job: container image, resource needs (VRAM, driver version, region), maximum price, verification policy and data-protection level.
  2. Escrow: the maximum price is locked in the L2 payment contract.
  3. The scheduler selects eligible nodes (Section 4.3).
  4. The node pulls the image, receives encrypted inputs, executes the job and returns outputs with an execution attestation.
  5. Verification runs according to the job's policy (Section 5).
  6. Settlement: if verification passes, the node is paid from escrow, the protocol fee is split (Section 7.1) and the node's reputation is updated. If it fails, the job is re-run on another node and the faulty node is penalized (Section 7.5).

4.3 Scheduling

Scheduling has two stages.

Stage 1: hard filters. A node is eligible only if it meets the job's minimum VRAM, driver and CUDA/ROCm versions, region constraints, confidential-computing requirement if any, minimum stake and minimum reputation.

Stage 2: scoring. Each eligible node is scored on normalized metrics. Each metric is converted to a percentile rank (0 to 1) among eligible nodes for that job class, so that different units can be combined fairly:

Score = w_f × N(TFLOPS) + w_v × N(VRAM) + w_b × N(Bandwidth) + w_l × N(Latency, lower is better) + w_r × N(Reputation)

The weights sum to 1 and are set per job class. Proposed defaults:

Job class TFLOPS VRAM Bandwidth Latency Reputation
Batch and rendering0.300.200.150.100.25
Inference0.200.150.100.300.25

Assignment is solved per scheduling window as a weighted matching problem that maximizes total score across all pending jobs and eligible nodes. To avoid concentrating work on a few top nodes, the final pick is drawn at random from the top-k scoring candidates. Weights and k are governance parameters.

5 · Verification and Trust

No single technique can verify every GPU workload, so OMC uses a policy selected per job.

Mechanism How it works Best for Cost to requester
Spot-check re-executionA random share of jobs (audit rate a) is silently re-run on an independent node and results are comparedAll deterministic or near-deterministic jobsBase price × (1 + a). Default a is proposed at 5% to 10%
Full redundancyThe job runs on 2 or 3 independent nodes and outputs are comparedHigh-value or high-precision jobs, chosen by the requester2× to 3×
Confidential computing (TEE)Job runs inside a hardware-protected environment with remote attestationSensitive data or proprietary models; requires supporting GPUsPremium for TEE-capable nodes
Zero-knowledge proofsCryptographic proof of executionResearch track; currently too costly for general GPU workloadsNot offered at launch

Handling non-determinism. GPU floating-point results can differ across devices and drivers. OMC therefore compares outputs using task-specific rules: exact hashes for deterministic jobs, numerical tolerance or perceptual metrics for rendering, and fixed seeds with deterministic kernels where inference supports it. Job types that cannot be verified reliably are labeled as such, and requesters are told before submitting.

Reputation. Each node has a score built from verified completions, uptime, timeouts and dispute outcomes. Scores decay without activity and cannot be transferred.

Disputes. A requester may dispute a result within 48 hours of the output being returned (proposed). The protocol first re-executes the job on an independent node. If the parties still disagree, the case escalates to L1 arbitration by a DAO-elected arbitrator set drawn from a pool of nine candidates, each posting a bond; a ruling is issued within seven days of escalation (proposed). Evidence (attestations, logs, hashes) is recorded on L2.

6 · Security and Privacy

Provider hardware is untrusted by default. OMC applies defense in depth, and is explicit about what it cannot guarantee.

Isolation. Jobs run in hardened sandboxes — gVisor by default, with higher-isolation tiers (Kata Containers or microVMs) selectable for sensitive workloads (proposed) — with no host access and no outbound network access by default.

Encryption. Inputs, outputs and images are encrypted in transit and at rest. Keys are released to a node only after it is assigned the job.

Data-protection levels. Standard (public or non-sensitive data), Protected (encrypted, ephemeral storage wiped after the job), and Confidential (TEE-only nodes).

Honest limitation. On non-TEE nodes, a malicious provider could in principle observe data or model weights in GPU memory. Requesters with proprietary models or regulated data should choose the Confidential level or not use the network.

Provider protection. Resource limits and abuse detection protect providers from malicious containers. Prohibited content and workloads are defined in the Acceptable Use Policy published on the official website.

Assurance. Smart contracts and the node client are audited before mainnet and the audit reports are public (see Section 11). A bug bounty program runs from mainnet, paying severity-based rewards starting at $50,000 for critical findings on a public bounty platform (proposed).

7 · Economics and the OMC Token

Parameters marked proposed in this section are design targets: they will be validated on testnet and may change through governance before mainnet.

7.1 Payments and Fee Flows

Requesters pay per job in OMC or in supported stablecoins (USDT and USDC on BNB Chain). Paying in OMC receives a 10% fee discount (proposed). A protocol fee of 3% is applied at each job settlement and is split as follows:

  • 30% of the fee is used to buy back OMC on the market and burn it automatically through smart contracts.
  • 70% goes to the Reputation Reward Pool, held by the Treasury contract and distributed to nodes in proportion to their reputation-weighted verified work.

The burn equals 0.9% of settled job volume (3% × 30%). As an illustration only, not a forecast: if monthly settled volume were $10 million, the monthly buyback and burn would be about $90,000. Actual volume, and therefore burn, depends entirely on network usage. The protocol publishes settled volume, fees, burns and emissions on a public dashboard.

7.2 Token Utility

  • Payment for compute jobs, with a fee discount for settling in OMC (Section 7.6).
  • Staking by node operators as security for honest behavior — the stake is simultaneously the right to accept work, the source of a buyer's refund and the discount qualification (Sections 7.5, 7.6).
  • Governance voting on protocol parameters, treasury use and upgrades.
  • Rewards to providers for verified work.

OMC has a fixed maximum supply of 1,000,000,000 tokens on BNB Chain (BEP-20). The only mechanisms that reduce supply are the fee buyback-and-burn and the slashing burn.

7.4 Emission Schedule for Node Rewards

The 500,000,000 OMC reward pool is released in equal annual instalments over 10 years. The ceiling is the same in every year, and within a year release is linear by day. There is no halving step and no epoch structure: a provider that joins in year 7 is mining against the same annual ceiling as one that joined in year 1.

The schedule is deliberately flat. A halving curve front-loads roughly three quarters of the pool into the first four years and then leaves late-joining providers with a fraction of the early rate; a flat curve keeps the reward rate for a given unit of verified work legible for the whole decade, which is what a provider buying hardware to run for several years actually needs to underwrite. The supply that is not released in the early years is not removed from the pool — it is simply released later, so the total is unchanged at 500,000,000 OMC over 10 years.

Emission is a ceiling, not an entitlement. Rewards are paid only against verified work. Daily emission is scaled by network utilization (proposed function: daily emission = ceiling × min(1, U / Utarget), where U = paid GPU-hours ÷ online available GPU-hours and Utarget = 60%). Any emission not distributed goes to an Emission Reserve that can only be burned or re-released by DAO vote. Because the annual ceiling is flat, an under-utilized network accumulates a smaller reserve per year than an exponentially decaying schedule would, and that reserve can only re-enter circulation by DAO vote. This ties new supply to real usage rather than to the calendar.

7.5 Staking and Slashing

Nodes must stake OMC before receiving jobs. The required stake is a published ladder: five hardware tiers, each with its own fixed minimum. The same ladder is used on the testnet and on mainnet, so a node that registers on the testnet is already positioned for the tier it will hold at launch.

Tier Hardware Minimum stake (OMC)
1Consumer GPU, up to 24 GB VRAM (RTX 3090 / 4090 class)20 (proposed)
2Workstation / professional GPU (RTX A6000, L40S class)100 (proposed)
3Data-center GPU, 80 GB class (A100 / H100 single card)500 (proposed)
4Multi-GPU data-center node (2–8 accelerators)1,000 (proposed)
5Cluster-scale deployment (multi-node)5,000 (proposed)

The steps are deliberately non-linear. Tier 1 is priced at one airdrop entry (20 OMC) and tier 2 at one full airdrop (5 × 20 = 100 OMC), so a wallet that maxes out the community airdrop can reach tier 2 without buying anything. Tiers 3–5 escalate hard, because a node at those levels is underwriting data-center and cluster-scale capacity and a proportionally larger slashing exposure — the tiers separate provider classes rather than pricing a small step up in hardware.

Amounts are proposed and adjustable by governance before mainnet. The USD value of a given tier therefore moves with the token price; the ladder fixes the OMC amount, not the dollar amount, and governance is expected to revisit the rungs if the token's price range changes materially.

7.6 The Compute Market: What the Stake Is Actually For

Staking only has meaning if the stake is doing work. In OMC it performs three concrete jobs, all of them enforced by a contract rather than by policy. The mechanism is the Compute Market — the buyer-facing half of the network. Suppliers register GPUs and stake; buyers post jobs and escrow payment. The market contract sits between them.

1. The stake is the right to accept work. A provider cannot be assigned a job unless its staked balance clears the hardware tier that job requires. The check reads the same ladder defined in Section 7.5 — the market contract does not keep its own copy. There is no separate reputation score gating assignment: eligibility is stake, and it is verified on chain at the moment of assignment. Below the tier minimum, the assignment reverts.

2. The stake is the source of the buyer's refund. When a job is created the payment is escrowed, not transferred. Settlement releases it only against an accepted result, and the contract settles at or below the buyer's stated ceiling, returning the difference. If a provider fails — missing the deadline, dropping offline mid-job, delivering output that fails verification — the penalty is taken from that provider's stake, never from another user's escrow, and the majority share is paid directly to the buyer. A refund is the seller's debt, not a redistribution of someone else's deposit. This is the property that makes the escrow trustworthy: the money that can pay the buyer back is locked before the job starts.

3. The stake earns a settlement discount. A buyer paying in OMC rather than a stablecoin receives a reduction on the protocol fee described in Section 7.1. The discount is a governance parameter, not a return: it changes what a buyer pays, not what a staker earns. It is deliberately modest, because an aggressive discount would convert a security deposit into a yield product and attract capital with no intention of running hardware.

Job lifecycle. A job moves through a fixed state machine: escrowed → assigned → delivered → settled, with disputed, refunded and cancelled as terminal branches. The buyer may cancel an unassigned job and recover the escrow in full. Once a result is delivered, the buyer has a 48-hour dispute window (Section 5) to contest it; uncontested results release automatically, so payment cannot be stalled indefinitely by buyer inaction.

Verification policy is chosen per job. Buyers pick the assurance level their workload justifies — spot-check re-execution of a sample, full redundant execution across independent nodes, or hardware attestation (TEE) — and the cost scales with it. This keeps cheap, low-risk jobs cheap rather than forcing every buyer to pay for redundant execution they do not need.

Scope at launch. The market is a testnet tool. It does not sell production compute and publishes no price list or rate; buyers quote their own ceiling and the arithmetic is shown, not an offer. Mainnet deployment follows the same schedule as staking (Section 10).

8 · Governance

Governance moves from team-led to community-led in stages.

Launch stage. Protocol upgrades are controlled by a 4-of-7 multisignature wallet held by core contributors, with a 3-day timelock (proposed). All changes are announced publicly before execution. Individual signers are not disclosed at this stage; the signing policy, key-management practice and any signer rotation will be published before mainnet.

Transition stage. OMC holders vote on parameters (scheduling weights, audit rates, fee split, slashing sizes, emission scaling) and Treasury spending. Proposed thresholds: a proposal threshold of 100,000 OMC, a quorum of 10% of circulating supply, a 7-day voting period and a 2-day execution delay.

Full DAO stage (2027 or later). The multisig is removed or limited to emergency security actions with transparent, time-limited powers.

Treasury reports are published quarterly.

12 · Risk Factors

Technical risk. Verification methods may fail on some workloads, smart contracts may contain bugs despite audits, and the L2 rollup may have availability or security issues.

Security and privacy risk. Non-TEE nodes cannot fully protect data in use. Sandbox escapes and side-channel attacks are possible.

Market risk. Token prices can be extremely volatile and may fall to zero. Liquidity may be thin. Unlock events may add selling pressure.

Adoption risk. The network needs enough requesters and providers. If demand is low, reward emissions are not matched by fee revenue and the burn is small.

Competition risk. Established clouds and other decentralized networks may offer lower prices or better reliability.

Hardware and supply risk. GPU prices, availability, export controls or energy costs may affect the provider base.

Regulatory risk. Laws on tokens, data protection and cross-border services may change and may restrict use in some countries.

Governance risk. Voting power may concentrate, and early-stage control by a multisig carries key-management risk.

Dependency risk. OMC depends on BNB Chain and third-party infrastructure.

This list is not exhaustive.
