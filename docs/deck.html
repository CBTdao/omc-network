Executive summary

Three ideas behind OMC

01

Honest scope

Inference, single-node fine-tuning, batch rendering and other embarrassingly parallel jobs — not frontier-scale cluster training.

02

Verification you can price

A per-job policy: spot-check re-execution, optional full redundancy, TEE where available, staking and slashing. The buyer chooses the cost of certainty.

03

Demand tied to usage

Fees are paid per job: 50% burned, 30% to stakers, 20% to the treasury. Emission is capped by trailing fee revenue, so supply growth is a function of demand.

Problem

Compute is scarce, expensive and centralized

Demand

AI developers face high hourly prices, regional quota limits and vendor lock-in. Studios face sharp render peaks they cannot size hardware for.

Supply

Consumer GPUs, small data-center idle capacity and post-PoW hardware sit underused — most of it for most of the day.

The gap

There is no widely trusted way for strangers to rent GPU time with enforceable guarantees about correctness, privacy and payment.

The answer

OMC matches a job to a staked node, holds the payment in escrow, verifies the result by policy, and pays only on settlement. The stake is what the buyer gets back if the result is wrong.

Why it is not just a marketplace. A marketplace without staking is a forum with a wallet: the default is a dispute nobody can price. Every node posts a bond in USD terms, and slashing pays the wronged party, so a bad result has a cost that is known before the job is submitted.

The first three are the demand side and the last is supply. A network that only has the fourth is a token with a dashboard, which is why the fee split pays stakers only from settled volume.

Architecture

Scheduling in two stages

Stage 1 — hard filters

VRAM, driver and CUDA/ROCm version, region, confidential-computing requirement, minimum stake and reputation. A node that cannot run the job is not a candidate.

Stage 2 — weighted scoring

Each metric becomes a percentile rank among eligible nodes. A weighted matching problem maximizes total score, and the final pick is drawn at random from the top-k.

Design choiceWhy
Random pick from top-k, not the single bestA deterministic winner is a target for capture and a fixed job history for its owner.
Percentile ranks, not raw scoresOne node with an unusual GPU cannot dominate every category.
Reputation is earned from settled jobsIt cannot be bought, and it is slashed with the stake.

Verification and trust

No single technique verifies every workload

MechanismHow it worksCost to requester
Spot-check re-executionA random share of jobs is silently re-run on an independent node; a mismatch triggers slashing+5–10%
Full redundancyThe job runs on 2–3 independent nodes and outputs are compared2× – 3×
Confidential computing (TEE)Hardware-protected execution with remote attestation, for inputs that cannot be re-runTier-dependent
Stake and slashingThe floor under all of the above: a wrong answer costs the node its bondIncluded
What this does not claim. Deterministic proof of arbitrary GPU work is an open problem, and this deck does not say otherwise. The product is a priced probability of correctness, chosen per job — not a claim that every result is provably right.

Security and privacy

Explicit about what cannot be guaranteed

Isolation

Hardened sandboxes — gVisor by default, Kata microVM tiers for sensitive workloads. No host access, no outbound network by default.

Encryption

Inputs, outputs and images encrypted in transit and at rest. Keys are released only after assignment.

Three protection levels

Standard, Protected (ephemeral, wiped after the job) and Confidential (TEE-only nodes).

On a node the operator physically controls, isolation is a mitigation, not a proof. That is why the protection level is the buyer's choice and is priced: a workload that cannot tolerate the residual risk should select Confidential and pay for it.
